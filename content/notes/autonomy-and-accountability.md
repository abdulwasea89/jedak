A person delegates a task to a software agent. The agent performs the work, perhaps across several applications, and returns a result. The person can stop participating in the intermediate steps, but the result still belongs to a real environment: a repository, a business process, a customer relationship.

The distinction between delegating execution and transferring responsibility becomes important when an action has consequences beyond the conversation. A model can produce a plausible explanation for an action without being an institution capable of owning its consequences.

## The boundary appears in ordinary work

Consider a routine business workflow that sends a reminder to a customer. Automating the preparation of the message is relatively straightforward. Automating delivery introduces questions of permission, accuracy, timing and what to do when a customer has already responded.

These questions exist with ordinary software too. AI makes them more visible because the system may choose a sequence of actions that was not individually specified in advance. The organization still has to establish which actions fall within the delegated task.

A useful authorization is concrete. It specifies resources, permitted operations, a time window and any conditions that require approval. A vague instruction to optimize a business process is not a sufficient boundary for arbitrary changes to that process.

## Human oversight is a design problem

Requiring a person to approve every tool call defeats much of the purpose of delegation. Removing approval everywhere creates a different failure: the human becomes aware of consequential actions only after they occur.

The relevant distinction is the cost of error and the reversibility of an operation. A read-only search can usually run with less supervision than a financial transaction. A local draft can be edited; sending it to thousands of customers changes the environment in a way that cannot be fully undone.

Good oversight relies on information quality. An approval should show what is about to happen, why the system proposes it and what assumptions remain unresolved. Asking someone to approve a vague summary merely moves a button press to the human without improving their ability to decide.

## What remains human work

Some work involves selecting an objective rather than executing a procedure. Organizations must decide which outcomes matter, whose interests count and what risks they are willing to accept. Automation can inform those decisions and carry out the resulting policies. It does not remove the need for them.

The distribution of gains from automation is also a social decision. Greater throughput can benefit customers, employees, owners or some combination of those groups. The technology does not determine that distribution by itself.

## Designing for the relationship

An agent system can be evaluated partly by how well it maintains this relationship between execution and responsibility. Its actions should remain attributable; its authority should be limited; its failures should be recoverable where possible. The people responsible for the process should be able to inspect and alter the rules.

Autonomy is useful precisely because it reduces the amount of execution a person must supervise. Accountability is what makes that reduction sustainable when the work matters.
